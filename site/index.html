Can you rank a 4v4 from wins and losses?

I wanted to know who the strongest Rage Forest players were. That sounds like a normal rating problem until you remember that every game has eight players, eight random civilizations and only one result.

This is how I tried to solve it, what did not work, and the resulting list.

FIRST: WHAT IS RAGE FOREST?

Black Forest, turned into its own game.

Rage Forest grew from a group of people who enjoyed playing 4v4 Black Forest together. The Discord gave those regular games a community and, eventually, their own name. There is a lot of walling, booming, trading, fish booming, flaming and, occasionally, actually attacking the other team.

It is also different enough from the ranked ladder to reward a different set of skills. Timings, team compositions, adapting to huge map variations, working with three team-mates and understanding eight civilizations at once all matter. A brilliant Arabia player is not automatically a brilliant Rage Forest player, or the other way around.

This is not an attempt to give the community an official or permanent ladder. I wanted to find out whether the match history contains enough information to rank RF players accurately, then use that method for my 2026 list. I will probably revisit the question next year rather than keep a live ladder running.

THE RESULT

The 2026 rankings

The rating is an estimate, not a precise measurement. The uncertainty column shows how much room there is for that estimate to move simply because we have limited evidence. ±100 is normal, so two nearby players should not be treated as definitively ordered.

Technically, the number is one standard deviation. A useful rough reading is that about two thirds of the plausible range sits within that distance of the rating.

THE METHOD, FROM THE BEGINNING

Start with two players

I will start with the familiar 1v1 version, then add the problems that appear when eight players share one result.

The easy case: chess and Elo

Suppose a 1600 player beats a 1500 player. Elo already expected the stronger player to win, so the ratings move a little. If the 1500 player wins, that is surprising and the ratings move more.

This works beautifully for chess because one player beats one player. You know who deserves the credit, and Elo can update the two ratings after every game.

Now make it a 4v4

Four players win and four lose. Was player A carrying? Did B have the perfect civilization? Were E and F much stronger than their team-mates? The result does not say. Giving the same credit to all four winners is convenient, but it is not a good description of what happened.

There is another problem. Rage Forest is not one pool of strangers. People return to the same hosts and the same groups. A player going 50–50 inside one circle may be excellent, average, or weak compared with everyone outside it.

WHERE BRADLEY–TERRY COMES IN

A model for learning hidden strength from simple choices

The Bradley–Terry model was published by Ralph Bradley and Milton Terry in 1952. It was made for paired comparisons: if A and B are compared, how likely is A to be preferred over B? Give every item a hidden strength and let the difference between those strengths determine the probability.

A win is the same kind of information. In a 1v1, the model asks which two player strengths make all the recorded wins and losses most plausible. Unlike Elo, it does not have to close each case forever as it arrives; it can fit the whole history together.

THE USEFUL PART

Reopen every old case

Imagine you lost repeatedly to someone two years ago. Later, that player starts beating the best people in the community. Elo does not go back and reconsider your old losses. They are done.

A global fit does reconsider them. It sees your games, their later games and everyone connected to both of you. It can conclude: yes, you lost, but you lost to someone much stronger than we knew at the time, so those losses should not count against you as heavily.

For a 4v4, each team is represented by the average of its four player strengths. The model then finds the set of player ratings that makes all 87,813 results most likely. That is the starting point for the 2026 list.

WHY THE GROUPS MATTER

First rank people within a crowd. Then place the crowds.

Rage Forest is not one well-mixed pool. Many players return to the same hosts and team-mates, while some groups rarely meet at all.

HOW THE CROWDS ARE FOUND

Start with who actually plays together

I made a network with one point per player and stronger links between people who were repeatedly in the same lobby. A clustering method then looks for parts of that network with many links inside the group and fewer links going out. Nobody was assigned a crowd by country, language or my opinion.

The first pass found the rough groups, but one of them became a 3,964-player leftovers bucket because a few extremely active players connect to almost everybody. I refined that result with a method that accounts for some players simply having far more connections than others. The largest group fell to 1,017 players, and the revised groups predicted later matches better.

Two separate questions

Suppose Group A plays hundreds of games among themselves. Those results can tell us who is strongest inside Group A. They cannot tell us whether an average Group A player should be rated 1400, 1700 or 2000 compared with Group B.

The games between the groups answer that second question. If Group A regularly beats Group B, the model moves Group A's shared level upward. A player's estimate is then their own result within the group plus that group level.

This also gives players with limited evidence a more sensible starting point. Instead of pulling everybody towards one global average, the model pulls them partly towards the level of the people they actually play with. Players with many games depend less on that help.

ONE FIT, THREE PARTS

Players, civilizations and crowds are estimated together

For each match, the model builds a score for both teams from three pieces:

  1. Players: the average of the four player terms on each side.
  2. Civilizations: an adjustment for the eight civilizations drawn in that game. This affects what the result tells us; it does not permanently add a good civilization to somebody's rating.
  3. Crowds: a shared level for each regular playing group. The player rating shown in the table already combines the player's individual term with this crowd level.

All three are fitted at the same time. If a team wins with a strong civilization draw, the model does not have to give all of that credit to the players. If somebody goes 50–50 in a very strong crowd, the model does not have to call them average.

HOW AN IDEA EARNED ITS PLACE

Learn from the past. Judge it on the future.

A model can always explain the games it has already seen more closely by becoming more complicated. The real test is whether that complexity helps on matches that came later.

01

Separate training games from test games

I fit each candidate on older matches, then froze it and predicted a later period. Those later games are called “held out” because the model was not allowed to learn from them. I repeated important tests across several time periods; one lucky result was not enough.

02

Count correct favourites

Accuracy is the simple score: if the model says Team A is more likely to win and Team A wins, that is one correct call. It is easy to understand, but it treats a cautious 51–49 prediction exactly like a confident 90–10 prediction.

03

Check whether the confidence was sensible

Log-loss fills that gap. A wrong 51–49 call costs a little; a wrong 90–10 call costs a lot, because the model was confidently wrong. Lower log-loss is better. On this page, a negative change means an idea improved the predictions, a positive change means it made them worse, and a change near zero means it added no useful information.

WHAT ELSE WENT INTO THE FINAL VERSION

The awkward parts of real match history

Each rule below was either checked on later matches or added to prevent the model from treating bad records as normal games.

01

Alternative accounts

One person can appear under several names, and two people can share a name. I resolved confirmed accounts before fitting. This matters: one missed merge can split a player's evidence and put the same person in the table twice.

02

Recent evidence matters more

People improve, decline and return after long breaks. Match weight halves after one year. Removing that decay cost 0.65 and 0.29 percentage points of prediction accuracy on two held-out periods; a very short 45-day memory was clearly worse. I also adjust for the average age of each player's evidence, rather than pretending two equally rated records are equally current.

03

Games ending inside five minutes

On Rage Forest, that is normally a drop, restart or argument rather than a completed contest. I tested several rules by fitting on older games and scoring later, normal games. Excluding the sub-five-minute games improved log-loss by 0.0031 and 0.0051 on the two test periods. I also checked whether stronger players were less likely to lose them. In normal games that relationship was strong: the correlation was −0.64, where −1 would mean that higher rating lined up perfectly with fewer losses. Inside five minutes it was only −0.12. The “winner” of these games was barely related to playing strength. Putting them back even with the duration adjustment was measurably worse.

04

The five-to-ten-minute grey area

These games are less clear. Some may still be drops or restarts, but unlike the first five minutes, they can also contain a real—if very short—contest. The choice was whether to count them fully, count them partly or throw them away.

I compared five short-game rules on two later test periods. Against discarding everything under ten minutes, the one-fifth rule was slightly worse in the first period (−0.0031 versus −0.0038 log-loss), but that difference was smaller than the measurement noise. In the second period it was clearly better (−0.0051 versus −0.0027). Giving five-to-ten-minute games full weight was worse than one fifth in both periods.

This is not a slam-dunk result: the exact ordering shifted as the corpus changed. One-fifth stayed because it won one test clearly, lost the other only within noise, and matches the evidence that these games contain some information but less than a normal game.

05

Longer games carry a little more information

A win remains the main result, but match length helps distinguish a close game from a one-sided one. Teams the model saw as even averaged 48.7 minutes; the most uneven games averaged 41.7. I graded the training result slightly by duration and tested it across five time windows. It improved log-loss by 0.00102 overall and worked in four of five.

06

Was match length really helping?

First I ran a placebo test. I kept every result and every match length, but randomly reassigned the lengths among games with the same winning side. The calculation was unchanged, but “quick win” and “slow win” were now random labels. The improvement disappeared. That shows the original ordering by match length contained real information.

I also removed duration completely when predicting the later test games, and the improvement remained. Duration was only helping the model learn ratings from past games; it was not being allowed to peek at the length of the game it was predicting.

One caveat remains. Adding the duration calculation also slightly weakens the pull that moves uncertain ratings towards the average. Compared with another model where I weakened that pull by the same amount but ignored match length, duration's extra benefit was small and uncertain. So match length clearly contains information about how decisive a result was, but I cannot say exactly how much of the prediction gain came from that information alone.

DOES IT PREDICT GAMES?

65.8% correct. What does 67.1% mean?

The test games come after the training games. The model does not get to learn from the future and then “predict” the past.

I compared the model with two familiar alternatives. Elo is the simple rating update from the earlier example, adapted to use team averages. TrueSkill is Microsoft's system for rating players in team games; unlike Elo, it also keeps track of how uncertain each rating is. I tuned both fairly and scored all three on the same held-out period.

Elo predicted 62.86% correctly, TrueSkill reached 64.28%, and this model reached 65.78%.

A rating is trying to estimate each team's real chance, not claim that the favourite must win. If a perfectly rated game is genuinely 50–50, either prediction will still be wrong half the time. If another game is genuinely 70–30, always choosing the favourite gets it right seven times out of ten, not ten times out of ten.

I repeated that calculation with every probability from the 9,256 held-out matches used for the video: take the favourite's chance in each match, then average those chances. The result was 67.08%. In other words, if teams given a 70% chance really won seven times out of ten—and the same held at every other percentage—the model's current range of confidence would produce about 67 correct calls per 100 games. It actually made 65.78.

This is a ceiling for the range of probabilities this model currently produces, not proof that no future model could pass 67%. Reaching 70% while keeping those confidence estimates honest would require team gaps about 1.2 times as spread out as they are now—roughly 207 rather than 173 Elo—and some real extra signal to justify that confidence. Balanced lobbies naturally keep the number low.

THINGS I TRIED AND LEFT OUT

Interesting ideas that did not work

Each idea below starts with the ordinary question it was meant to answer, then explains the test and the result. A pattern in old games was not enough; it had to help with later ones.

These tests happened throughout the project, so they are not all on the final 87,813-match snapshot. The latest set run under one shared test setup used a corrected 82,607-match development set; a few older negative checks were not rerun after later data repairs. I treat those as project history rather than perfectly comparable scores, and I did not quietly re-select old ideas after seeing the final refresh.

LOOKED REAL IN OLD GAMES, DID NOT HOLD UP

Recent form and streaks

The question: if somebody has recently won more than their normal rating expected, are they “in form” and likely to keep over-performing?

What I tried: I calculated a separate recent-form adjustment from older games, then used it on later ones. I also tried an intentionally unfair upper bound that could peek at some results from the test period itself.

What happened: the honest version helped in one test period but not the other. Even the version that peeked at answers added at most 0.40 percentage points of accuracy. No separate hot-hand rating shipped; only the general time decay and evidence-age adjustment remain.

NO REPEATABLE EFFECT ACROSS 2,094 PAIRS

Specific team-mate chemistry

The question: do two particular players win more together than their individual ratings suggest?

What I tried: I split the games into ten batches. Each time, player ratings came from nine batches and pair chemistry was checked on the untouched tenth. This covered 2,094 repeated pairs. I also ran a stricter version that removed a pair's shared games before estimating either player.

What happened: both tests found an average pair effect essentially equal to zero. The data could not support thousands of separate chemistry ratings.

THE FOUR-PLAYER AVERAGE WON CLEARLY

Weakest link or strongest carry

The question: is a team really worth the average of its four players, or does its weakest player drag it down—or its strongest player carry it?

What I tried: I gradually tilted the team score towards the weakest player, then repeated the test while tilting it towards the strongest. “Soft minimum” and “soft maximum” are just the mathematical names for those two sliding scales.

What happened: predictions became steadily worse as I moved away from the ordinary average in either direction. Averaging the four ratings won clearly.

EVERY EXTRA LOBBY FEATURE WAS WORSE

Trying to describe the lobby around the ratings

The question: can information around the ratings explain something the ratings themselves miss?

What I tried: I compared the teams' experience, recent activity, form, evidence age, time played together, number of crowds represented and variety of team-mates. I tested each alone, all together, and with a more flexible method that could find combinations such as “experience matters only when activity is low.”

What happened: some patterns looked impressive in the old games, but every version was worse on later ones: +0.0030 log-loss together and +0.0039 for the flexible version. They were describing noise in the fitted ratings, not a repeatable advantage.

REAL HINTS, WORSE PREDICTIONS

Civilizations changing by patch or player level

The question: should Spanish have one value for the whole four-year history and for every skill level? Balance patches and the players using a civilization could change how strong it is.

What I tried: I estimated separate civilization adjustments across seven patch eras, and another set that could change with the level of the lobby. Spanish, for example, ranged from +1.8 to +5.7 percentage points across eras.

What happened: the smaller groups of games made those extra estimates too noisy. Patch-specific civilization ratings worsened later log-loss by 0.00032; lobby-level versions worsened it by 0.00056. The movement may be real, but this dataset cannot estimate it safely enough to improve predictions.

EXTRA MATCHUP TERMS MADE THINGS WORSE

Rock-paper-scissors player styles

The question: a single rating says that if A is stronger than B and B is stronger than C, A should usually beat C. Could play styles create a circle where C is unusually good against A?

What I tried: I added extra matchup values that could learn who tends to over-perform against whom, beyond the normal rating difference. The first version was deliberately limited to a small number of broad matchup patterns so it could not simply memorize every named pairing.

What happened: even that limited version worsened later log-loss by 0.00073. Versions flexible enough to learn many specific counters fitted the old games extremely well and failed badly on new ones. The apparent counters did not repeat.

SIX DIFFERENT RULES, NO IMPROVEMENT

Shrinking experienced players differently

The question: uncertain ratings are pulled towards a safer average. Should that safety pull fade faster for somebody with thousands of games?

What I tried: I tested six rules that changed the strength of the pull according to game count, from giving experienced players more freedom to pulling them towards the middle more strongly.

What happened: none improved later predictions. Pulling the most active players in harder was clearly worse, so the simpler shared rule stayed.

THE ALTERNATIVES LOST ON LATER GAMES

Different ways to find crowds

The question: there is no official list of Rage Forest crowds, so which automatic grouping of the same player network should the model trust?

What I tried: the first method found the rough groups but left a 3,964-player catch-all because a few very active people connect almost everybody. I refined it with a method that accounts for those unusually busy connectors, then compared it with three other network-grouping methods. Leiden also searches for groups with many internal links. Spectral clustering turns connection patterns into positions and groups nearby players. Infomap looks for areas where an imaginary walk through the network would tend to stay for a while.

What happened: the refined groups improved predictions again across seven new test periods. The three alternatives were worse by 0.00030, 0.00107 and 0.00556 log-loss respectively.

NO GAIN FROM REPACKAGING THE SAME INFORMATION

Adjusting confidence and blending models

The question: perhaps the model chooses the right favourite but says 70–30 when it should say 60–40. Or perhaps Elo and TrueSkill make different mistakes that could cancel out when their predictions are averaged.

What I tried: three methods adjusted confidence without changing the underlying ratings. Temperature scaling makes every prediction uniformly softer or stronger. Platt scaling learns a simple smooth correction from old predictions. Isotonic calibration allows a more flexible staircase-shaped correction. I also made an “ensemble”—simply a weighted blend of this model, Elo and TrueSkill.

What happened: two confidence adjustments changed almost nothing and isotonic calibration was worse. The model blend was also exactly neutral: the systems disagreed, but not in a reliably useful way.

DOUBLING THE DATA BOUGHT 0.05 POINTS

More matches as the answer

The question: is the model stuck near 66% simply because it has not seen enough games yet?

What I tried: I trained the same model on one eighth, one quarter, one half, three quarters and then all of the available history. A separate data refresh later recovered another 5,165 games.

What happened: accuracy rose from 62.2% with one eighth of the data to 65.0% with half, then stayed at 64.9–65.0% as the rest was added. The extra 5,165 games moved it by only 0.04 percentage points. More data still improves coverage and narrows uncertainty, but it no longer solves prediction accuracy by itself.

HOW TO READ THE LIST

It is a model of the recorded games, not a verdict on a person

The model sees who played, which civilizations they had, when the game happened, how long it lasted and who won. It does not see voice communication, map position, connection problems, substitutions, who hosted, or whether somebody was experimenting with a silly build.

Flank and pocket clearly matter, but reliable positions are not present in the match records. That was not a failed model idea; it was something I could not test without guessing the input first.

It also assumes everyone is trying their hardest. In Rage Forest that is, occasionally, a heroic assumption. There is no column for “couldn't be asked to wall today.”

Win rate is not a second rating. Strong players can have ordinary win rates because hosts give them weaker teams. The team, opponent and lobby averages in the table help show the level of games behind each result.

To enter the ranked pool, a player needed at least 50 effective games. “Effective” means that five-to-ten-minute games count one fifth and games inside five minutes count zero. They also needed a game in the previous 100 days and enough games between their crowd and other crowds to place that crowd on the common scale. If any confirmed account merge is missing, please tell me; those corrections matter.
