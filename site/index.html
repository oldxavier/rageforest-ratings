FIRST: WHAT IS RAGE FOREST?

Black Forest, turned into its own game.

Rage Forest grew out of Age of Empires II's Black Forest map and a Discord community built around 4v4 games. There is a lot of walling, booming, trading, fish booming, flaming and, occasionally, actually attacking the other team.

It is also different enough from the ranked ladder to reward a different set of skills. Timings, team compositions, reacting to a strange map, working with three team-mates and understanding eight civilizations at once all matter. A brilliant Arabia player is not automatically a brilliant Rage Forest player, or the other way around.

These games are unranked, so there is no official Rage Forest ladder. That is the gap this project is trying to fill.

Why ranks stop at 500

All 765 eligible players are listed. The first 500 keep their exact rank and rating. The remaining 265 are alphabetical and all show the same broad position (501–765) and rating range (7xx–14xx).

That is deliberate. The estimates become noisy enough down there that publishing an exact order would make the table look more certain than it is. You can still see games, win rate, uncertainty and the three lobby averages.

THE METHOD, FROM THE BEGINNING

Start with two players

The final model is complicated. The basic idea is not.

The easy case: chess and Elo

Suppose a 1600 player beats a 1500 player. Elo already expected the stronger player to win, so the ratings move a little. If the 1500 player wins, that is surprising and the ratings move more.

This works beautifully for chess because one player beats one player. You know who deserves the credit, and Elo can update the two ratings after every game.

Now make it a 4v4

Four players win and four lose. Was player A carrying? Did B have the perfect civilization? Were E and F much stronger than their team-mates? The result does not say. Giving the same credit to all four winners is convenient, but it is not a good description of what happened.

There is another problem. Rage Forest is not one pool of strangers. People return to the same hosts and the same groups. A player going 50–50 inside one circle may be excellent, average, or weak compared with everyone outside it.

WHERE BRADLEY–TERRY COMES IN

A model for learning hidden strength from simple choices

The Bradley–Terry model was published by Ralph Bradley and Milton Terry in 1952. It was made for paired comparisons: if A and B are compared, how likely is A to be preferred over B? Give every item a hidden strength and let the difference between those strengths determine the probability.

A win is the same kind of information. In a 1v1, the model asks which two player strengths make all the recorded wins and losses most plausible. Unlike Elo, it does not have to close each case forever as it arrives; it can fit the whole history together.

THE USEFUL PART

Reopen every old case

Imagine you lost repeatedly to someone two years ago. Later, that player starts beating the best people in the community. Elo does not go back and reconsider your old losses. They are done.

A global fit does reconsider them. It sees your games, their later games and everyone connected to both of you. It can conclude: yes, you lost, but you lost to someone much stronger than we knew at the time, so those losses should not count against you as heavily.

For a 4v4, each team is represented by the average of its four player strengths. The model then finds the set of player ratings that makes all 87,813 results most likely. That is the core of this ladder.

WHAT HAD TO BE ADDED

Rage Forest is not just four names against four names

Each addition below solves a specific problem in the data.

01

Alternative accounts

One person can appear under several names, and two people can share a name. I resolved confirmed accounts before fitting. This matters: one missed merge can split a player's evidence and put the same person in the table twice.

02

Civilizations

Rage Forest normally assigns eight different civilizations at random. That makes their effects measurable. Spanish, for example, won 54.8% of 5,941 games. Replacing an average civilization with Spanish has about the same model effect as replacing one of the four players with somebody rated 162 points higher.

03

Crowds

The co-play network contains 22 groups. I pool players partly toward the average of the group they actually play in, rather than pretending every player comes from one perfectly mixed ladder.

04

Connections between crowds

A crowd can only be placed on the shared scale through games against outsiders. In this dataset, 89 high-volume players account for half of ranked cross-crowd player-games. That number describes where the connecting game volume comes from; it does not identify 89 uniquely special “bridge people.”

05

Time

People change. Games lose half their weight after one year, which tested better than either a much shorter or longer memory. The final rating is also adjusted for how old a player's evidence is.

06

Short and decisive games

Games at or below five minutes are excluded. Five-to-ten-minute games count only a little. Longer games keep the win as the main result, with a small duration-based signal for how decisive it was.

DOES IT PREDICT GAMES?

About 66%—and 67% is close to the limit

The test games come after the training games. The model does not get to learn from the future and then “predict” the past.

On the same held-out period, tuned Elo predicts 62.86% correctly, TrueSkill reaches 64.28%, and this model reaches 65.78%.

Why not 80% or 90%? Because a fair game cannot be predicted. If two teams are truly even, calling it 50–50 is the correct answer, even though the eventual winner makes the prediction look “wrong” half the time.

I estimated the ceiling from the sharpness of the games themselves. Even with the ratings exactly right, the expected hit rate is about 67.08%. Hosts are deliberately making balanced lobbies, so low predictability is partly evidence that they are doing their job.

THINGS I TRIED AND LEFT OUT

More complicated was often just more complicated

An idea only stayed if it helped on later games the model had not been fitted on.

Recent form and streaks

Some versions looked useful in one time window and disappeared or changed sign in another. They were not stable enough to publish.

Specific player chemistry

Pairs of team-mates simply do not play enough distinct, comparable games for thousands of separate chemistry effects to hold up.

Position and role guesses

Flank and pocket clearly matter, but those positions are not reliably present in the public match data. Guessing them would create a confident-looking variable that is often wrong.

More data as the answer

Recovering another 5,165 games moved accuracy by 0.04 percentage points. It improved coverage and uncertainty, but the prediction score was already saturated.

HOW TO READ THE LIST

It is a model of the recorded games, not a verdict on a person

The model sees who played, which civilizations they had, when the game happened, how long it lasted and who won. It does not see voice communication, map position, connection problems, substitutions, who hosted, or whether somebody was experimenting with a silly build.

It also assumes everyone is trying their hardest. In Rage Forest that is, occasionally, a heroic assumption. There is no column for “couldn't be arsed to wall today.”

Win rate is not a second rating. Strong players can have ordinary win rates because hosts give them weaker teams. The team, opponent and lobby averages in the table help show the level of games behind each result.

To enter the ranked pool, a player needed at least 50 effective games, a game in the previous 100 days, and enough games between their crowd and other crowds to place them on the common scale. If any confirmed account merge is missing, please tell me; those corrections matter.
