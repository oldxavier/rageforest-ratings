THE PROBLEM

A 4v4 win is only one bit of information.

In chess, one player beats one player. In Rage Forest, eight people arrive with eight civilizations, uneven roles, familiar team-mates, and lobbies deliberately balanced near 50–50. The result tells us who won—not which player caused it.

The only way through is to stop rating matches one at a time. Fit all games globally, and ask which set of player strengths best explains every result at once.

THE METHOD

From lobby to leaderboard.

The released fit is a regularized Bradley–Terry model: logistic regression expressed in rating points, with safeguards added only after they survived held-out tests.

  1. 01

    Resolve who is who

    Alternative accounts are merged before fitting. One person must be one parameter; display-name decisions never become model evidence.

  2. 02

    Encode each lobby

    Four player coefficients and four civ coefficients enter positively, the opposing eight negatively. Swapping teams flips the prediction exactly.

  3. 03

    Fit every game together

    Ridge regularization keeps sparse players from flying to absurd ratings. A one-year evidence half-life lets the ladder describe current strength.

  4. 04

    Respect the social graph

    Community pooling stabilizes groups that mostly play internally. A crowd without enough cross-crowd evidence is listed but not ranked on the shared scale.

  5. 05

    Grade decisive results

    The win remains the central bit. Match duration adds a lightly weighted margin signal; games under five minutes are excluded and five-to-ten-minute games are discounted.

  6. 06

    Publish uncertainty

    Ratings are not exact measurements. The table exposes one-SD uncertainty and requires 50 games, recent activity, and a placeable crowd.

HONEST EVALUATION

Most of the available signal—not most games.

A coin flip starts at 50%. On the same held-out footing, tuned Elo reaches 62.86%, TrueSkill 64.28%, and this model 65.78%. The model's own confidence implies a practical ceiling near 67.08% because good lobbies are designed to be uncertain.

Adding another 5,165 games moved accuracy by just 0.04 percentage points. More data still improves certainty and coverage; it no longer changes many binary predictions.

WHAT DIDN'T WORK

The graveyard matters.

A failed idea is evidence when it was tested on data the model had not seen.

Recent form

Rolling streak and form features looked tempting in-sample, then failed to repeat consistently across future time windows.

NOT SHIPPED

Player chemistry

Pair-specific team-mate effects were too sparse and unstable to beat regularized player strength out of sample.

NOT SHIPPED

More network cleverness

Several richer interaction and community corrections either moved calibration without improving predictions or depended on the test period.

NOT SHIPPED

Read this leaderboard as a model, not a verdict.

  • Only observed public match outcomes are measured. Communication, effort, role, connection problems, substitutions, and who hosted are not.
  • Win rate is descriptive, not strength: stronger players can have ordinary win rates because hosts balance teams around them.
  • The “89 bridge players” figure means 89 high-volume players account for half of ranked cross-crowd player-games. It measures activity concentration, not a unique social role.
  • Identity resolution can be wrong. Corrections are welcome and are recorded rather than silently overwritten.
